Shattered Porcelain and the Reckoning

At a formal family dinner, architect Elena refuses to surrender the apartment she owns. Her husband smashes a plate across her face, believing public violence will force her to comply. Instead, the broken porcelain becomes the first piece of evidence in a financial, familial and legal reckoning.

Fiction. Contains intimate-partner violence, blood, physical injury, financial coercion, family betrayal and legal proceedings.

Chapter One: The White Tablecloth

I never imagined that my own husband would try to take away everything I have in front of his entire family.

When I refused, he grabbed a dinner plate and smashed it over my head. The plate shattered on my face.

Blood trickled down my neck and I stared into his eyes.

“You cannot know what I can do.”

Everyone at the table froze instantly.

My name is Elena Ward Brooks.

I am thirty-eight years old, an architect and the founder of Northline Design, a Minneapolis firm specializing in historic preservation and adaptive reuse.

Jackson stood up abruptly, his face flushed with anger and expensive red wine.

“How dare you reject my mother, you selfish woman?”

We were having dinner at his parents’ estate in Beverly Hills, surrounded by nearly two dozen members of the Brooks family.

Just an hour ago, the night had seemed perfect.

Roast lamb.

Crystal cup.

fresh flowers.

Polite conversation about school, holidays and charity dinners.

Next, Grace Brooks rested a manicured hand next to her wine glass.

“I decided to live in Elena’s apartment in Minneapolis for a while,” she announced.

Not a discussion.

It’s a decision.

She said stairs in California are becoming increasingly inconvenient.

My apartment occupies the top floor of a renovated warehouse, overlooking the Mississippi River.

I bought it four years before I met Jackson.

Even the renovation design was done by myself.

It was where I rebuilt my life after losing my father.

This is also the place where I founded “Northern Design” with a folding table by the window.

It was there that Jackson later proposed to me.

Grace had been there once and complained that the kitchen was too modern.

And now, she spoke as if the key had been put in her handbag.

“I need a housekeeping allowance of twelve hundred dollars a month,” she continued, “and Jackson said this would be the most convenient.”

I looked at my husband.

He was still concentrating on cutting the meat on the plate.

“When did we discuss this?”

“Aren’t we discussing it now?”

“No. Your mother is making a unilateral announcement.”

Grace sighed.

“This defensiveness is really exhausting.”

Jackson’s brother, Aaron, leaned back in his chair.

“Since the apartment is empty, why not let mom live in it?”

“It’s not available.”

“You live in California now,” Grace said.

“I have jobs in both cities.”

“Jackson is your husband. His family should benefit from this marriage.”

Around the dinner table, relatives nodded in agreement.

Before anyone asked the owner for permission, my apartment became a public asset of the family.

Then, Grace pushed a leather folder in front of me.

Inside was an occupancy agreement.

An authorization letter for monthly transfers.

A governing document that gives Jackson the authority to “maintain, refinance, lease or dispose” of the property as necessary.

I closed the folder.

“No.”

Only one word.

Grace put her hand on her chest.

Jackson stared at me as if I had betrayed him.

His father, Harold, lowered his head.

Then Jackson started ranting.

He called me selfish.

Cold-blooded.

Unfaithful.

He said that I relied on the advantages his family gave me to build my current life, even though “Northern Design” existed before I met him.

I remained firmly seated in my chair.

“That apartment belongs to me. I will not transfer it and I will not support your mother.”

His hands tightly grasped the dinner plate in front of him.

A cousin sitting next to the child quietly led them toward the hallway.

Jackson raised the plate.

No one stopped him.

Plates flew across the table.

The porcelain hit me hard on the left side of my head with a crisp sound that echoed under the chandelier.

Hot mushroom juice splashed into my hair.

Broken pieces of porcelain lay scattered on Grace’s white tablecloth.

For several seconds, my ears were ringing and I couldn’t hear anything.

Before my legs gave out and I fell to my knees, my hands firmly grasped the edge of the table.

Blood poured from the wound near my temple and stained the linen tablecloth.

No one gasped.

No one stood up.

No one came to help.

This deathly silence is more chilling than the pain of a plate being hit on the head.

I pulled out a sharp piece of porcelain from my curls and stood up slowly.

Jackson still looked furious.

No fear.

No sorry.

He firmly believed that violence had concluded this negotiation.

“You cannot begin to know what I’m capable of,” I told him.

Grace carefully lowered the cleaver.

I took my phone out of my bag.

The screen was covered in blood but still functional.

I called 911.

“Good evening,” I said, “I need the police and an ambulance. My husband attacked me in front of his entire family.”

Jackson’s expression changed.

I continued.

“There were approximately twenty witnesses at the scene. The address is 1840 C2 Street, North Quince, Beverly Hills.”

Grace stood up.

“Elena, put the phone down.”

I looked at her.

“The homeowner is registered as Harold Brooks. My attacker is Jackson Brooks.”

Jackson walked around the table.

“Hang up.”

I backed away.

The dispatcher asked on the other end of the line, “Ma’am, is the suspect approaching you?”

“Yes.”

Harold finally stood up.

“Jackson, stop.”

It was the first useful thing he said tonight.

Six minutes later, police sirens sounded in the distance.

During this time, three relatives had already tried to leave quietly.

A camera in front of the gate recorded the license plate of each vehicle.

The restaurant’s cameras recorded much more.

Jackson didn’t know this yet.

Grace doesn’t know either.
