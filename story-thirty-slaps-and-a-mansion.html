Thirty Slaps and a Mansion

After his adult son Ryan strikes him thirty times at a birthday gathering, sixty-eight-year-old builder Leonard Mercer stops making excuses. Surveillance footage, forged loan documents and a long-prepared audit turn the mansion Ryan flaunts as his own into the centerpiece of a reckoning over abuse, fraud and a father's misplaced protection.

Fiction. Contains elder abuse, graphic physical violence, blood, family betrayal, financial fraud, coercion and legal proceedings.

Chapter One: I remember every slap

I recorded every slap. 1. 2. Three. When my biological son slapped me hard on the face for the thirtieth time, my mouth was filled with the smell of blood, my vision began to blur, and all the excuses I had made for him were completely wiped out then.

He thought he was humiliating an old man. But what he didn’t know was that at the moment he took action, I had decided to bury the bustling place where he was standing with my own hands.

My name is Leonard Mercer. I am sixty-eight years old. Over the past four decades, I have built commercial towers, housing developments, logistics parks and highway projects throughout California. Before my son learned to tie a tie, I had spent countless sleepless nights in a construction trailer, surviving bankruptcies, betrayals, lawsuits, union strikes, recessions, corrupt inspectors, and failed partnerships.

This is the story of how I uprooted my son’s mansion and sold it completely while I sat drinking coffee and thinking I owned the world. The story begins on a cold February evening. I drove to Beverly Hills for my son’s birthday dinner.

I parked three blocks away because the driveway was already packed with luxury cars—imports rented by a group obsessed with showmanship. In my hand I held a gift simply wrapped in brown paper. It was Ryan’s thirty-second birthday.

From the outside, the mansion looked immaculate. That’s how it should be. I paid for every inch of it. Six years ago, after completing the largest transaction of my career, I purchased the property outright through a holding company in my name.

After Ryan and his wife, Vanessa, got married, I let them move in. They always thought it was their own property. I never corrected them. Because for them, it is a natural reward. For me, that was a test.

And they blew the test beautifully. The warning signs emerged several years ago.

Ryan stopped calling me “Dad.”

Vanessa insisted that I text before coming to the door. They laughed at my old pickup truck, my faded jacket, and my rough hands—the same hands that supported the luxurious lifestyle they showed off on social media.

At the party, they introduced me as some kind of has-been businessman who got rich by accident.

“Leon got lucky when the real estate boom happened,” Vanessa once laughed in front of her friends.

This always makes me laugh. There’s no such thing as mixing concrete at four in the morning. Shit Luck is not going to negotiate with the union. No luck sleeping in a cold construction trailer while a multi-billion dollar project hangs by a thread.

I built an entire business empire that my son is pretending to understand. But that night, everything exploded over a small incident. I gave Ryan an antique Rolex that had been restored in the 1960s—the same model that his grandfather had dreamed of during his lifetime but could not afford until his death.

Ryan didn’t even bother to look at it. In front of everyone, he threw the gift box aside.

“I’m tired of you acting like we owe you something,” he yelled. “This house is no longer any of your business.”

The whole place fell silent. Vanessa was sitting on the sofa with a glass of red wine in her hand. Her friends stared silently at their cocktail glasses. One of Ryan’s cronies in the financial world gave a gloating sneer, as if he had paid for a ticket to see a private circus.

I looked at my son calmly.

“Be careful, son. Don’t forget who is laying the foundation for the ground you stand on.”

That’s enough. Ryan stood up suddenly, his chair falling backwards with a clang. Then he pushed me. A hard push. Before anyone else could react, he slapped them. Another slap. Again and again. Vanessa didn’t stand up.

She made no sound to stop him. She didn’t let him stop either. She sat there with a vicious smile hanging on the edge of the red wine glass, as if she was enjoying a cold joke that only she understood.

I silently recorded every slap. Not because I’m weak. It’s because with every blow, something inside me completely dissipates. Love. Hope. Fantasy. When he finally stopped, Ryan was breathing heavily, like a victor who thinks he’s won.

I slowly wiped away the blood from the corner of my mouth. I stared straight at my son. At that moment, I understood the most painful truth every father can learn. Sometimes, you don’t raise a grateful child.

You just funded an arrogant stranger. I didn’t scream. I didn’t threaten him. I didn’t call the police in his foyer. I bent down and picked up the gift box from the floor. The vintage Rolex inside was safe and sound.

Then I turned around and walked out. No one followed. Without my son. Without his wife. Not a single guest eating food I paid for under my crystal chandelier. When I walked outside, the cold air hit my face, causing the wounds on my face to hurt.

I sat in the pickup for several minutes, hands gripping the steering wheel. The cheeks were beating violently. My jaw aches. There were specks of blood on the collar of his shirt. But my mind was clearer than ever.

Soberer than I have been in years. I drove to an urgent care clinic in Westwood. The nurse asked me if I had fallen.

I almost blurted out “yes”.

The instinct of trying to protect the child is still stirring in the body, even if the child has become so cruel. Then, I saw my reflection in the dim glass window. Red and swollen cheeks.

Cracked lips. Blood oozed from the corners of his mouth. He is sixty-eight years old, but he is still lying about the traitor who beat him with his own hands at the birthday banquet, while his wife was still smiling.

“No,” I said, “my son was beaten thirty times.”

The nurse stopped typing. She raised her head and her eyes changed. That was the look in the eyes of a professional medical staff. A look that doesn’t ask me to downplay the truth. She documented everything.

Take photos. Document bruises. Lacerations. Redness and swelling. Possible minor concussion. She asked me if I wanted to call the police.

I said, “No need for now.”

Immediately, I changed my mind.

“No, call the police. But I also need a full medical report.”

At 2:04 in the morning, after finishing the notes, I walked out of the clinic with a folder in my arms. It’s two-thirty-one and I’m sitting alone in my little house in Pasadena. Not a mansion.

Not a glass office. Nor is it the fake life that Ryan has painstakingly cultivated online. My true abode. An old two-bedroom Craftsman-style house with scuffed floors, a study filled with old blueprints, and the air filled with the scent of sawdust from the cedar cabinets I was refinishing for my own amusement.

I put the Rolex case on the kitchen table. Then, I took out my cell phone and dialed my lawyer’s number. Elaine Porter answered on the fourth.

“Leonard?”

“My son attacked me tonight.”

There was dead silence. Immediately, there was the sound of chairs scraping the ground.

“Is it seriously injured?”

“It’s heavy enough.”

“Are you safe?”

“Safe.”

“Is there a medical report?”

“In my hand.”

“Are there any witnesses?”

“A roomful of cowards.”

“Is there surveillance?”

I looked at the folder that contained ownership records for all the holding companies in my name.

“Yes,” I said, “there are surveillance cameras everywhere in that mansion.”

Ain breathed a sigh of relief.

“Then try to get some sleep. At eight o’clock, we will officially start work.”

I looked at the antique Rolex. I wanted to give my son a piece of history. As a result, he restored my sobriety.

“No,” I said, “we’ll start now.”
