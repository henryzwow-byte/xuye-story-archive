FICTIONAL STORY · A-075

The Truth in the Recording

While her husband is deployed, his family invades the home she owns, assaults her and accuses her of marrying for money. They believe no one will challenge their version of events—until a recording preserves every threat, insult and lie.

Fiction. Contains domestic violence, physical assault, harassment, coercion, family conflict and legal proceedings.

Chapter One: Slapping and Recording

My mother-in-law slapped me so hard that I hit the wall of the corridor. Before I knew I’d fallen, I had a mouthful of blood. The room suddenly fell into dead silence.

Not the peaceful silence that comes with the normal evening calm of the dishwasher buzzing and the porch sensor lights automatically turning on. This is the kind of dead silence where the whole room waits with bated breath for someone to break the awkward silence after crossing the line.

But no one spoke. The crystal chandelier overhead was trembling slightly, casting golden light on the heart-leaf hardwood floor that Daniel and I had renovated two years ago. My shoulder bumped against the photo frame hanging by the stairs. It hung crookedly now, and the glass was cracked, just across the face of Daniel, who was wearing a navy dress.

Behind me, Tessa let out a snort of disgust. Then she got close enough that I could smell her vanilla perfume and that pungent white wine scent.

“Vampire,” she spat, as if I were some kind of dirt scraped from the sole of a shoe.

Marcus laughed on the couch in the living room. He had one leg crossed over his knee, Daniel’s whiskey glass in his hand, and his muddy boots stomped on our coffee table. Daniel moved that coffee table home from a weekend garage sale because he said it looked like the one in his grandfather’s study.

“Daniel’s still overseas, sweetie,” Marcus said. “No one’s coming to save you.”

I put a hand over my cheek. Hot pain exploded beneath the skin. My ears were ringing, and for a moment the world around me sounded like it was separated by a thick layer of water.

Gloria Hale stood condescendingly before me, wearing a cream silk blouse, a pearl necklace around her neck, and that “church luncheon smile” she wore whenever she wanted to pass cruelty off as concern. My mother-in-law spent sixty-two years in the same circles in a small Southern town where people still praise someone who destroys you with a smile as “graceful.”

“You only married him for his military benefits,” her voice was low and clean, “for his pension. For this house.”

I slowly raised my eyes. This house.

This red brick duplex is located at the end of a quiet cul-de-sac. There is a magnolia tree planted in the front yard. Next to the HOA (Home Owners Association) mailbox on the corner, neighbors always like to exchange all kinds of gossip in the name of pleasantries. I found this house when Daniel was still renting a shabby bachelor apartment near the base with only a few folding chairs in the kitchen. This house was the down payment I paid myself before getting married. I renovated this house little by little with the money I earned from consulting. At that time, his family still ridiculed me for being so lucky.

This is the house that Daniel insisted on being in my name - as he said when he signed the transfer papers: “Before this happened, you were my home.”

I didn’t say the words. It’s not time yet. Tessa crossed her arms and dug her bright red nail polished fingers into the sleeves of her suit jacket. She is Daniel’s sister, but when she needs money, she behaves like a wounded little girl in desperate need of her mother’s protection; and when she is rejected, she transforms into a sisterly and maternal figure.

“Daniel should have married a well-matched woman,” she said, “not an office rat who only laughed and signed.”

This almost made me laugh out loud. Office mouse. For the past six years, I have been working as a forensic financial investigator. Not the bookkeeper. Not an assistant. Nor was she the timid woman they imagined she would be on Thanksgiving, rushing to refill iced tea before anyone could say anything.

When money disappears without anyone noticing, companies often pay me a fortune to help.

I can follow companies with friendly names and uncover shell accounts hidden behind them; I can find forged signatures in the cracks of regular supplier contracts; I can find invoices that have been paid twice, loans issued in the names of deceased relatives, and charity funds laundered through church charity sales - because these thieves love to pretend to be innocent and kind.

I’m as proficient at financial lies as some women are at their go-to recipes. And for the past three months, I have been secretly investigating my own parents-in-law. At first, I told myself I was mistaken.

That’s the most embarrassing part. I wanted so badly that I was wrong that I rationalized every tiny anomaly until the excuses weighed more heavily than the truth itself. At 2:14 in the morning, a login reminder came from Daniel’s overseas deployment account.

A supplier invoice in the name of the Harbor Grace Foundation had my electronic signature on it, but I had never seen the document. A credit inquiry was initiated using Daniel’s military identification number from a bank that was not in the county where we lived.

A text message from Gloria asking Daniel if he still had “that special veterans account,” followed by three little dots that flashed and disappeared without a trace.

I saved all the evidence. I didn’t tell anyone at first. I just watched. This is my area of expertise. Quiet people are always looked down upon because they never chime in in group conversations. People always forget that we are still sitting in the corner, still listening, still silently watching which drawer they open and which name they deliberately avoid.

Gloria took a step forward and picked up a thick white folder held by a silver binder clip from the entryway table. Those neat blue index labels look familiar to me, because I also classify them according to this rule when organizing documents at home.

She pressed the folder against my chest.

“Tomorrow morning,” she said, “you go to the notary’s office on Willow Pike. Transfer half of the house to Marcus and give Tessa access to the savings account. Daniel won’t know until it’s done.”

My hands gripped the edge of the folder.

“Do you think Daniel will be indifferent when he finds out that half of his savings is missing?”

Marcus scoffed. “He trusts his mother.”

“He trusts me,” I said.

Gloria’s eyes turned cold.

“He just pities you,” she replied. “There’s a fundamental difference.”

This sentence just hit an old wound in me. Not on the face. It’s a deeper place.

For years, I carried homemade pies and casseroles into the Heyer family holiday parties, harboring some foolish hope that one day I would no longer feel like a visitor being evaluated. Gloria never made a scene in public. The methods she used were even more sinister. She would correct my recipes at church potlucks, point to my shirt and ask if I got it during the sale season, and would even introduce to strangers that I was “particularly good at handling documents” - making it seem like I was not Daniel’s wife, but his personal secretary.

Marcus borrowed Daniel’s truck and each time he returned it, the gas tank was empty. Tessa asked for favors, discounts, testimonials, donations, and referrals. Gloria always said one thing: a family should help each other.

But in the Haier family, “family” always means: Daniel is responsible for giving, and the rest are responsible for taking.

I tried to be kind because Daniel loved them dearly. Then I tried to be patient because he was on assignment overseas. Then, funds began to mysteriously disappear. Then, there are forged signatures. Then came the unusual alert from the bank.

Finally, I remember the slap that landed firmly on my face. My phone vibrated in the back pocket of my jeans. At first I didn’t move. Marcus looked at me with that lazy smile he wore when he thought he was sure of victory. Tessa looked at the folder in my hand, her eyes so greedy that she seemed to have seen the jewelry and mountains of cash in the cabinet from every signature line.

Gloria leaned forward.

“If you dare to embarrass this family,” she threatened in a low voice, “I guarantee that when Daniel comes back, he will face a mentally unstable crazy woman.”

My phone vibrated again. I put one hand in my pocket, pretended to lean against the wall to steady myself, and glanced down at the screen. Daniel. Land early. Arrive home in ten minutes. Don’t act rashly. I brought witnesses.

Tonight, for the first time, my breathing rhythm changed. The margin is small. But it was enough to make my lungs remember how to work again. I wiped the corner of my mouth with my thumb. There was a faint trace of blood on his fingertips. I looked at Gloria, then Tessa, and finally Marcus, who was slumped comfortably on the couch, drinking Daniel’s whiskey.

“You’d better get out of here before Дэниел (Daniel) comes back,” I said.

Marcus threw his head back and laughed. This was his first fatal mistake.
