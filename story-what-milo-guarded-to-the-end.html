FICTIONAL STORY · A-013

What Milo Guarded to the End

At his owner's rain-soaked funeral, a dying golden retriever turns on the new priest and exposes the stolen keepsake—and the quiet dignity—he has spent his last strength protecting.

Fiction. Contains death, a funeral, grief, theft, an animal in distress and the peaceful death of an elderly dog.

Chapter One: He Only Wanted to Walk Beside Him One Last Time

Cold November rain fell over Saint Andrew Valley Cemetery. An unpainted pine coffin rested beside the open grave. The damp air carried the mingled smells of resin, soil and decaying leaves. A dozen mourners stood beneath black umbrellas, their shoes slowly sinking into gray mud.

Yet the hardest sight to bear was not the freshly dug grave. It was the old dog beside the coffin. His name was Milo, a ten-year-old golden retriever. In his youth, Milo’s coat had looked like honey warmed by the sun. Now the fur around his muzzle and eyes had turned completely white. His thin frame disappeared beneath a dull, tangled coat, and every difficult breath made his ribs rise and fall.

Three days earlier, his owner, Walter Hale, had been found dead in the old armchair inside his cabin. Since that day, Milo had refused every bite of food and every drop of water. When the funeral attendants carried Walter’s body out of the cabin, Milo repeatedly threw himself against the door. The howl forced from deep in his throat made the neighbors turn away, unable to keep watching.

On the day of the funeral, no one put him on a leash. He was too weak to run. He could only drag his heavy body step by step behind the black hearse. His head hung low and his paws kept sinking into the muddy water, but he never stopped.

When the brief farewell service began, Milo suddenly did something no one expected. He lifted both front paws onto the open edge of the coffin. The first time, his hind legs slipped on the wet grass and his body fell hard. The second time, he failed again.

“Come back, Milo,” Walter’s niece Emily said as she crouched beside him, her voice already breaking.

But Milo did not look at her. A third time, he placed his paws on the coffin and spent the last of his strength pulling himself over the rough pine edge. He dropped gently inside.

He squeezed into the narrow space between Walter’s left arm and the coffin wall, then rested his gray-white head on his owner’s chest. His tail tapped the wood twice, slowly. Then he closed his eyes and released one long, exhausted sigh—like a child lost for too long in a storm who had finally found his way home.

“We should lift him out,” Emily said, wiping her tears. “He won’t survive like this.” Her husband, Daniel, gently touched her shoulder. “Let him stay,” he said, looking at Milo inside the coffin. “He is leaving too. He only wants to sleep beside his owner for a while. Let them have this last bit of time together.”
