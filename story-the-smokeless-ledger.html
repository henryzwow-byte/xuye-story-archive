FICTIONAL STORY · A-042

The Smokeless Ledger

Claire Moretti wakes in a burn unit after a fire kills her mother and leaves her father weeping beside the bed with immaculate cuffs. Pretending that smoke erased her memory, the forensic accountant quietly joins Detective Lena Ortiz and her partner Julian Price to follow the records her mother hid before her death. Insurance amendments, secret payments and a Northline risk file reveal that the blaze was not an accident, but the final move in a financial conspiracy. Claire must turn the ledger into testimony before her father destroys the last evidence and rewrites the family tragedy as his own rescue story.

Fiction. Contains arson, the death of a parent, severe burns, medical trauma, financial fraud, intimidation, attempted destruction of evidence, arrest and courtroom proceedings.

Chapter One: The Clean Cuffs

When I woke up, the first thing I tasted was smoke. It’s not the smell of smoke in my memory. It’s the smell of real smoke. It lingered on the tip of my tongue, in the back of my throat, and beneath the heavy gauze wrapped around my ribs. Every breath felt like swallowing ashes and broken glass, and it hurt.

The ceiling above me is white. Dazzlingly white. That’s standard hospital white. The monotonous beeping sound of the monitor came from somewhere to the left. Something pulled at my arm. My lips were terribly chapped, and the skin beneath the gauze and medicine burned in patches.

Then I heard my father cry. At first, the sound nearly sent me over the edge.

Because I’ve never heard Grant Moretti cry like that. In my mind, my father was a man who viewed tears as a “show of weakness” that others used to win arguments. He didn’t cry when I graduated from college, he didn’t cry at my grandmother’s funeral, and he didn’t even shed a tear when my mother was diagnosed with a benign tumor after a horrific three-week wait.

But now, he buried his face in his hands, sat beside my hospital bed, and shook violently.

“Dad,” I tried to say.

But he could only make a faint breath sound. His head shot up.

“Claire.”

When the name came out of his mouth, it sounded strange. Too weak. It looked too much like a rehearsed line. He held my hand carefully, as gently as if the entire ward was watching us.

And in fact, it is indeed the case. Two nurses stood at the end of the bed. A uniformed police officer was guarding the door. A doctor wearing a light blue surgical gown was looking at my father with professional sympathy.

Father leaned down and got very close.

“She didn’t make it out,” he whispered in a choked voice, “your mother, she... Claire, I’m so sorry. You’re the only survivor.”

This sentence emptied my whole being. For a moment, the severe pain in his body disappeared in front of an even greater nothingness. My mother. My memory began to dissolve into fragments. She stood at the kitchen island in a green dressing gown.

Turn towards the basement door. The first dull sound came from under the floor. The windows flashed with orange firelight. She screamed my name at the top of her lungs. Back door. It was locked.

That door shouldn’t have been locked. I tried to sit up. The burning pain tore my ribs. The nurse rushed over in one stride.

“Don’t move, honey. Relax.”

My father bent over my hand.

“I tried my best,” he sobbed. “By God, I tried my best to save you both.”

Everyone in the room heard it. Everyone in the room saw a husband who had lost the love of his life. A heartbroken father. A man who narrowly escaped death in a catastrophe but hated himself for being powerless.

His acting is impeccable. Grant Moretti has always been good at performing pain when witnesses are needed. I almost believed it. Not even close. However, my eyes accidentally fell on the cuffs of his shirt.

Snow white. Crisp. Spotless. No soot. No scorch marks. Not even a trace of smoky gray marks remain. Not even a tiny blister could be found on my wrist. Under the dazzling lights of the hospital, his expensive watch reflected a cold light.

There’s no way a man who claims he ran into a fire to rescue his wife and daughter will come out looking like he just returned from a yacht luncheon. My throat tightened. I stared at his cuffs until he noticed.

He shrank his hands slightly and pulled his shirt sleeves back into his suit jacket. At that second, Suspicion officially broke into the ward and sat down next to Sadness.

“Claire,” he said, “you’re still in shock. Get some rest.”

He squeezed my hand. It’s a bit strong. Just big enough for me to notice, but not big enough to attract the attention of others. A warning. The father stood up and turned to look at the doctor.

“I have to make the funeral arrangements,” he said. “My wife deserves the last dignity.”

The doctor nodded gently.

“We will continue to monitor her condition.”

My father looked back at me. His eyes are red and swollen. His cuffs were spotless.

“I’m right outside.”

He turned and left the ward. For several seconds, no one said a word. Then the uniformed officer stepped back. A middle-aged woman wearing a dark suit jacket came out of the corner behind the curtain.

I hadn’t noticed her before. She was about forty-five years old. Her black hair was tied tightly into a bun on the back of her head, and her eyes were so sharp that she seemed to be able to see everything without anyone’s permission.

She walked to my bedside and kept her voice low.

“Claire Moretti?”

I blinked.

“It’s me.”

“I’m Detective Lena Ortiz.”

My heartbeat suddenly accelerated. She placed three pictures on the blanket, just within sight of my burned hand that I dared not move. The first photo is of a melted fuel can in what looks like our basement stairs.

The second photo shows signs of prying around the gas valve. The third photo is of my father’s black car leaving our neighborhood at 9:41 p.m. That was eleven minutes before the first 911 call.

Detective Ortiz stared straight at me.

“He told us he was in the house,” she said, “but he wasn’t even there.”

The whole room seemed to tilt. The beeping of the heart monitor suddenly quickened. My eyes moved from the photo to the door where my father had just disappeared. My mother is dead. My father lied.

And under the fog of morphine, grief, burns, and smoke, I, who had been a forensic accountant for ten years, finally came to my senses. My father used to laugh at my work.

He called it “fiddling with little forms.”

But he forgot that numbers don’t cry. Numbers don’t panic. Numbers don’t forgive. Numbers will just wait. Detective Ortiz leaned forward.

“There’s more evidence,” she said, “but first I need to know what you remember.”

Before I could answer, my father’s furious roar suddenly came from the corridor.

“She is delirious! I obviously wanted to save them!”

Two police officers stepped into view and blocked him from the door. His face was full of tears. His cuffs were still spotless. Ortiz stared at him silently. Then she turned back to me. The pain is still there.

The sadness is still there. But something colder than them has taken root in my heart.

“Tell him,” I whispered, “I don’t remember anything.”

Ortiz’s eyes were as sharp as a knife.

“What else?”

I stared over her shoulder at my father.

“Tell him I believe everything he says.”
