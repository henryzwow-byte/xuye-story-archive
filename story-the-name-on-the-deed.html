FICTIONAL STORY · A-070

The Name on the Deed

Laura's family mocks the online business that has quietly paid their taxes, repairs and bills. When her favored sister returns with a doctor husband and tries to drive Laura out of the family home, one old deed reveals who truly owns the house—and who has merely been allowed to live there.

Fiction. Contains family abuse, physical assault, financial exploitation, property conflict, harassment and legal proceedings.

Chapter One: Occupation at the dining table

My name is Laura Bennett. I am thirty-one years old now, and for most of my adult life, when my family introduced my career to outsiders, they always referred to it disparagingly as “playing around on the computer.”

This is quite funny, because the money I earned from “playing around” paid my family’s property taxes.

It was replaced with a brand new heater. It paid for the renovation of the kitchen, which I had immediately redone after Frank nearly tripped over old tiles that were cracked.

It traded for an accessible walk-in shower, wider aisles, grab bars by the back door steps, and half of those grocery bills that my mom always complained about when I “got the wrong thing.”

I run an online application software business from home. It started when I was twenty-two years old as a simple scheduling tool I wrote for local service workers. Paid features, licensing, technical support contracts, and quiet growth followed—not a serious job in the eyes of my family, because in their eyes, success had to be a white coat, a court badge, or a beautifully framed diploma on the wall.

My sister Mary only recognized social status. She has been the beautiful, socially capable, and most favored daughter of her family since she was a child. Since she married a doctor, James Whitaker, my mother acted as if our family’s bloodline had been directly genetically upgraded.

Mary moved out three years ago and has rarely been back home since. Until last Thursday, she suddenly came back. She strode into the house wearing a cream coat, gold earrings and a sneer that could cut twine.

“Oh, Laura,” she looked me up and down, “you’re still here.”

I try to be polite.

“How are you doing? How is James?”

“Very busy,” she said coldly, “All truly promising careers are like this.”

My mother laughed. My dad stared at the phone screen without saying a word. This is our family’s daily routine. Mary humiliated me, my mom polished it into humor, and my dad was invisible in the air.

The next morning my mother ordered me to clean the whole house thoroughly because Mary and James were coming back for supper.

“They have important things to discuss,” she said.

I should have asked at that time: Why do we need to keep the house spotless when discussing things? But I actually already have the answer in my heart. The last time Mary came back, she had opened the doors to each room, checked the appliances, looked at the renovated bathroom, and felt the granite countertops with her hands as if she were appraising a second-hand property.

James said little during dinner. He looked tired, much thinner than before, and the expensive watch on his wrist looked empty. Mary plays the leading role in the whole scene. She complimented my mom on the barbecue, ignored my greetings, and occasionally made a little joke about how comfortable people who “work” in their pajamas are.

After dessert, my mother’s tone changed.

“Sit down, Laura. We’re not done yet.”

Mary folded her hands on the table as if she were presiding over a board meeting.

“I need you to give up the master bedroom.”

I blinked.

“What?”

“James and I are going to be living here for a while,” she said smoothly. “He’s going to work at a nearby hospital. The master bedroom is perfect for us.”

“That’s my room.”

Mary smiled.

“You don’t need it. You’re not married and you don’t have children. What else can you do but hide in there all day?”

My face burned.

“I have a job.”

My mom sighed, as if I was embarrassing her.

“You can move to the den upstairs.”

“Just that room filled with clutter and without even a wardrobe?”

“You’ll get used to it,” my mother snapped.

“No,” I said.

The weight of this word is heavier than I expected. Mary’s smile disappeared. My mother was so angry that she pushed away her chair and stood up.

“What did you say?”

“I said no. I wouldn’t give up the master bedroom to Mary.”

My dad continued to stare at his phone. Mary leaned back in her chair, her eyes flashing coldly.

“See? I told you. She feels like she’s the master of the house.”

For a second, the room was silent.

Then I looked at Mary and said calmly, “That’s because I am.”
